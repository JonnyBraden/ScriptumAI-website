How law firms lose institutional knowledge when lawyers leave

15 July 2026 · 6 min read · Jonny Braden

Every firm has a few people who simply know things. They know that a particular counterparty always pushes on the same indemnity, and always folds if you hold firm on the cap. They know which precedent to reach for on a share purchase agreement, and why the warranty schedule reads the way it does. They know the client who needs every change explained, and the one who just wants it done.

None of that is written down. It lives in the heads of individual lawyers, and when one of them leaves, most of it leaves too. For a small or mid-sized transactional firm, that is not a minor inconvenience. It is the quiet erosion of the firm's single most valuable asset.

Why the knowledge disappears

The problem is not that firms are careless. It is that the normal way of working scatters knowledge instead of collecting it. Consider where a firm's real expertise actually sits:

  • In tracked-changes documents buried in individual inboxes
  • In the reasoning behind a redline, which is almost never recorded anywhere
  • In a senior lawyer's memory of how a negotiation went last time
  • In personal precedent folders that never make it to a shared system

When work happens over email, the output survives but the thinking does not. You keep the final document. You lose the far more valuable record of what was tried, what was conceded, what was won, and why.

Why smaller firms feel it most

A magic circle firm has deep precedent banks, dedicated knowledge management teams and enough deal volume that patterns are visible across the practice. A twenty-partner firm rarely has any of that. Its knowledge is more concentrated in fewer people, which means each departure removes a larger share of it.

That is the uncomfortable irony. The firms with the least formal knowledge infrastructure are the ones for whom losing a key lawyer hurts the most.

The final document survives. The reasoning behind it, the part that took years to learn, usually does not.

What it actually costs

The cost is not abstract. It shows up as a junior lawyer redrafting a clause from scratch because nobody knows where the good version lives. It shows up as a weaker negotiating position because the person who remembered how the counterparty behaves has moved on. It shows up in the weeks of ramp-up time every time someone new has to rebuild an understanding the firm already had and then lost.

How firms can hold on to it

The fix is not to ask lawyers to document everything. That has been tried for decades and it fails, because it asks busy people to do a second job on top of their real one. The knowledge has to be captured as a byproduct of the work itself.

That means the platform where drafting and negotiation happen should quietly record the things that matter: which clauses were chosen, which amendments were accepted or rejected, what positions were taken across a negotiation, and how those threads resolved. Done properly, the lawyer simply works, and the firm accumulates a structured, searchable record of its own expertise without anyone filling in a form.

When that record belongs to the firm rather than to any individual, a departure stops being a loss of knowledge. The person moves on. What they learned stays.
