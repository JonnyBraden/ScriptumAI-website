Fallback ladders: negotiating from a prepared position

27 July 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

Fallback ladders in contract negotiation are one of the oldest tools in a deal lawyer's kit, but most firms carry them only in partners' heads rather than in a form the whole team can use.

What a fallback ladder is

A fallback ladder is a prepared sequence of positions on a given clause. You open at your preferred position. If that is rejected, you move to your first fallback. If that is rejected, you move to your second. Each step is prepared in advance, with a clear sense of what is acceptable and what represents a genuine line.

The alternative is ad hoc negotiation, which is slower, less consistent and more dependent on the individual confidence and experience of whoever is on the call. An associate without a ladder may concede more than necessary because they are not certain what the firm's position is. A partner without one may give different answers on similar matters depending on how the deal is going that afternoon.

Why most firms cannot operationalise this

Building fallback ladders properly requires knowledge of how similar negotiations have resolved: what the counterparty typically asks for, where deals have historically landed and what language has been accepted before. That information exists in most transactional firms, distributed across files, emails and memory. It is rarely consolidated in a form that a deal team can use at the moment they need it.

Knowledge management exercises designed to solve this tend to produce documents that are not maintained and not consulted. The information is there in theory but not in practice.
