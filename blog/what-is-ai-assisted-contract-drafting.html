What is AI-assisted contract drafting? A guide for transactional lawyers

8 July 2026 · 7 min read · Jonny Braden

AI-assisted contract drafting is one of those phrases that means very different things depending on who is selling it. For a transactional lawyer trying to decide whether any of it is worth the time, the marketing does not help much. This is a plain guide to what the term actually covers, what it does well, where it falls short, and how to tell a useful tool from an expensive distraction.

A working definition

AI-assisted drafting means using a language model to help produce or amend contract text, with a lawyer reviewing and approving the output. The important part is the word assisted. The tool proposes, the lawyer decides. Anything that removes the lawyer from that loop is not assistance, it is a liability.

What it does well today

Used sensibly, the current generation of tools is good at a specific set of tasks:

  • First drafts from a template. Populating a known template with deal-specific detail, so nobody starts from a blank page.
  • Clause-level suggestions. Offering a starting point for a particular clause, which the lawyer then shapes.
  • Summarising a redline. Turning a marked-up return into a readable list of what changed and where.
  • Surfacing questions. Flagging where a draft needs a decision or a piece of information from the client.

The common thread is that each of these compresses time spent on mechanical work, and leaves the judgment where it belongs.

Where it falls short

It is just as important to be clear about the limits. A general-purpose model does not know your firm's house style, your preferred fallback positions, or the reasons a clause is drafted a certain way for a certain client. Out of the box it produces text that is plausible and generic, which is precisely the wrong combination in a contract. It will also state things with confidence that are subtly wrong, so every output has to be read as a draft by someone who could have written it themselves.

A general model gives you plausible and generic. In a contract, that is the wrong combination.

The question that actually matters

Given all that, the useful question when evaluating a tool is not how clever the AI is. It is this: does the tool get better at drafting the way your firm drafts, the more your firm uses it?

A tool that treats every draft as a fresh, isolated request will always produce generic output. A tool that learns from your corrections, remembers which suggestions your lawyers accept, and builds a picture of your precedents and positions will start to feel less like a generic model and more like a very fast junior who has read everything your firm has ever done.

How to evaluate a tool

If you are assessing options, a few practical checks separate the substance from the hype:

  1. Does it work where your lawyers already draft, or does it force them into a separate window?
  2. Is every AI output subject to human approval before it enters a document?
  3. Does it improve with use, or does it forget everything between sessions?
  4. Is your firm's data kept isolated, and does the knowledge it builds belong to you?

The last point is the one most often overlooked. A tool that improves by learning from your work is only valuable to you if that learning stays with your firm rather than being pooled with everyone else's.
