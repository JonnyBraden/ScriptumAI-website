How counterparty profiling gives deal teams an edge

27 July 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

Counterparty profiling for deal teams is one of the most underused sources of competitive advantage in transactional law. Most firms have the data. Very few have it in a form they can actually use.

What is in a counterparty profile

Every time your firm deals with a counterparty or their regular solicitors, you learn something. What do they push for on representations and warranties? Where have they held firm on indemnities? How many rounds does their typical negotiation take, and where does it usually land? What language have they accepted before?

That information is currently sitting in your completed deal files, in emails, in redline histories and in the memories of the lawyers who handled those matters. It is not organised, it is not easily retrievable and it is not systematically shared with the team starting the next deal against the same counterparty.

Why it matters in practice

A deal team that goes into a negotiation with a counterparty profile is not better at negotiating in some abstract sense. They are better prepared. They know which issues are genuinely important to this counterparty and which ones are opening positions. They know what has been accepted before. They can open with more confidence and concede less unnecessarily.

The effect is particularly marked for associates. A junior lawyer negotiating against an experienced counterparty team is at a disadvantage that better preparation can meaningfully reduce. A counterparty profile gives them access to the accumulated experience of everyone at the firm who has dealt with that party before.

Building profiles without a dedicated function

The obstacle for most firms is the infrastructure required. Building and maintaining counterparty profiles as a deliberate exercise takes time and requires someone to own the process. In a practice of 20 to 100 fee earners without a dedicated knowledge management function, that rarely happens consistently.

Scriptum captures counterparty information as a byproduct of normal drafting and negotiation work, without a separate data entry step. Each deal adds to the firm's profile of that counterparty automatically. Over time, the firm builds a genuinely useful intelligence base from the deals it has already done.
