Can Canadian lawyers use AI to draft contracts?

Published 26 November 2026·6 min read
The short answer

Yes, Canadian lawyers can use AI to draft contracts. No Canadian law society prohibits it, and guidance from the Law Society of Ontario and the Law Society of British Columbia treats first drafting as a legitimate use. The conditions are the ones lawyers already work under: understand the tool, protect client confidentiality, supervise the output and stay responsible for every clause that goes out.

Corporate and commercial lawyers in Canada are being offered more legal AI than ever, from broad platforms such as Harvey and Legora to drafting tools that sit in Microsoft Word, such as Scriptum AI and Spellbook. The professional question comes first, though. This guide is for transactional lawyers at mid-sized Canadian firms and for in-house teams. It sets out what the law societies have said, what that means in practice, and how to choose a contract drafting tool that fits those rules.

What have Canadian law societies said about AI?

They have issued guidance rather than new rules. By April 2024, the law societies of Alberta, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, British Columbia and Ontario had all published guidance on generative AI, applying the existing codes of professional conduct to it.

The Law Society of Ontario's white paper on licensees' use of generative AI, tabled in April 2024, lists preparing first drafts of documents among the ways lawyers can use these tools. It also sets out the risks, including the unintended spread of confidential client information where a provider uses inputs to train its models. The Law Society of British Columbia's guidance ties AI use to the competence duty in its Code, including understanding a tool well enough to use it properly. Lawyers outside those provinces should check their own law society, and Quebec lawyers the Barreau du Québec.

Which professional duties apply to AI contract drafting?

The same duties that apply to any drafting delegated to a junior or a precedent. The table below turns them into questions to put to a vendor before a pilot.

DutyWhat it means for AI draftingQuestion to ask the vendor
CompetenceUnderstand what the tool does, where it draws its language from and where it can go wrongWhere does the drafted language come from: our precedents or general model output?
ConfidentialityClient information must not leak or be used to train modelsIs our data ever used for training, where is it stored and who are your sub-processors?
SupervisionA lawyer must review and approve AI work, as with a junior's draftCan a partner approve output before it reaches the client or the other side?
CommunicationConsider telling clients how AI is used on their matterCan we explain, in a sentence, what the tool did on this document?
FeesCharges should reflect the work actually doneIs the price published, so we can model the cost per lawyer?

Does Canadian privacy law affect legal AI?

Yes. Personal information in a share purchase agreement or an employment contract is still personal information when it goes into an AI tool, so PIPEDA or the relevant provincial law applies.

In December 2023, Canada's federal, provincial and territorial privacy regulators published joint principles for responsible, trustworthy and privacy-protective generative AI. They are not written for law firms, but they make a sensible checklist: limit the personal information that goes in, know what the provider does with it, and be able to account for it. Get the vendor's answers in the contract, not just the sales deck.

How should a Canadian firm check AI-drafted contracts?

The same way it checks any first draft, with the same partner signing it off. Using AI does not move responsibility for the document from the lawyer to the vendor.

Check defined terms, cross-references and governing law first, since a draft built on another jurisdiction's language can look right and still be wrong for Ontario or British Columbia. Then read the commercial terms against the instructions. Our guide on how to check an AI-drafted contract has a step-by-step list.

The law societies have not asked lawyers to avoid AI. They have asked lawyers to stay lawyers while using it.

What should a mid-sized Canadian firm look for in an AI drafting tool?

A tool that makes the duties above easy to meet on a live deal. In practice, that means four things.

  1. It works in Microsoft Word. Contracts are drafted and negotiated there, with tracked changes.
  2. It drafts from your own precedents. That keeps the language Canadian, and the firm's agreed positions in place.
  3. A lawyer approves everything. Nothing goes to the client or the other side without sign-off.
  4. The knowledge stays with the firm. Positions agreed on each matter build a clause library the firm owns.

Common questions

Is it allowed for Canadian lawyers to use generative AI?

Yes. No Canadian law society bans it. Guidance from the Law Society of Ontario, the Law Society of British Columbia and others says lawyers may use generative AI, provided they meet their existing duties of competence, confidentiality, supervision and candour with clients.

Do I need client consent to use AI on a Canadian matter?

Not always, but you should consider it. Law society guidance asks lawyers to think about whether to tell clients how AI is used on their matter, particularly where confidential information goes into a tool or the use affects cost. Many firms deal with it in their engagement letter.

Who is responsible for a mistake in an AI-drafted contract?

The lawyer. Law society guidance is clear that using a tool does not shift professional responsibility. A lawyer has to review and approve every AI-drafted clause before it reaches the client or the other side.

What privacy rules apply to legal AI in Canada?

Canadian privacy law, including PIPEDA and provincial equivalents, still applies to personal information put into an AI tool. In December 2023 Canada's privacy regulators published joint principles for responsible, privacy-protective generative AI, which are a useful checklist when assessing a vendor.
