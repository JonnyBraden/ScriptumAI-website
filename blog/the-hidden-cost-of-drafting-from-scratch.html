Capacity

The hidden cost of drafting from scratch

9 August 2026 · 3 min read

The hidden cost of drafting from scratch goes well beyond the hours on a timesheet. For transactional practices that still treat every first draft as a new exercise, the real toll shows up in capacity, consistency and the quality of the lawyers' attention.

The time you can see

The visible cost is straightforward: an associate spending three hours on a first draft of a shareholders' agreement that a good precedent base could produce in forty minutes. Multiply that across a team of fifteen lawyers handling fifty transactions a year and you are looking at a significant number of fee-earner hours devoted to reconstruction work rather than the judgment and negotiation that clients are actually paying for.

Most firms know this cost exists. Fewer have calculated it, and fewer still have done anything systematic about it.

The time you cannot see

The less visible cost is inconsistency. When each lawyer drafts from whatever precedent they happen to have saved locally, the firm's output varies in ways that are hard to audit. One associate's limitation of liability clause may be materially different from another's, not because the facts called for it, but because their starting points diverged years ago and nobody noticed.

That inconsistency creates rework. Partners spend time harmonising drafts that should have started from a common base. Clients spot variation across documents and ask questions the firm cannot easily answer. The cost of this rework rarely appears anywhere in a time recording system.

The opportunity cost

The deepest cost is what the firm does not do because its lawyers are occupied with drafting reconstruction. A senior associate who spends most of Monday producing a first draft from scratch is not available to advise a client on a tight deadline, review a counterparty's markup, or develop the firm's position on a novel deal structure.

AI-assisted drafting from firm precedents does not eliminate this time entirely, but it moves the balance decisively. The lawyer still exercises judgment on the draft, still negotiates, still applies experience to the specific facts. What changes is the starting point: a solid, consistent first draft produced in a fraction of the time, drawn from the firm's own established positions.
