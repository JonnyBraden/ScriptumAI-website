The hidden cost of drafting from scratch

3 August 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

The hidden cost of drafting from scratch is not the time a lawyer spends writing. It is the time spent rediscovering what the firm already knows.

Every transactional practice has negotiated the same issues dozens of times. The IP ownership clause in a commercial agreement, the liability cap in a services contract, the anti-dilution mechanism in a funding round. The senior partner knows exactly where the firm's position is on each of these. The associate drafting at 9pm on a Tuesday does not, so they start from a blank page or a generic precedent and work outward from there. The output takes longer to produce and longer to review.

What starting from scratch actually costs

The direct cost is hours. A first draft that takes six hours to produce from scratch might take two hours to produce from a well-maintained precedent grounded in the firm's own positions. On a complex transaction with multiple documents, that difference compounds. A deal with eight key documents where each one takes twice as long as it should is a deal where the fee earner's time is materially misdirected.

The indirect cost is consistency. When different lawyers draft independently from different starting points, the firm's output reflects the knowledge of whoever happens to be drafting rather than the collective intelligence of the practice. A client who instructs you on ten matters over five years may receive subtly different positions depending on which associate handled each one. That is a reputational risk as much as an efficiency problem.

The precedent as the real solution

The case for a well-maintained precedent library is strong and has always been strong. The reason many firms do not have one is that building and maintaining it requires work that does not bill directly. Lawyers who know which precedents are good are too busy to update the ones that are not. Associates who might help do not always know enough to make good editorial decisions. The knowledge stays locked in individual files and individual heads.

Scriptum changes this dynamic by capturing institutional knowledge as a byproduct of the drafting work that is already happening. Each time a lawyer drafts from a precedent, reviews it, and works through a negotiation, the platform learns. The firm does not have to schedule a knowledge management project. The project happens inside normal work.

For a transactional firm where senior time is the binding constraint, eliminating the cost of starting from scratch on well-trodden documents is one of the fastest ways to add effective capacity without adding headcount.
