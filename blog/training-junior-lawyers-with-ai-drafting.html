How AI drafting speeds up training for junior lawyers

19 July 2026 · 3 min read

How AI drafting speeds up training for junior lawyers is a question that deserves more careful thought than "it does the drafting for them." The training value of AI assistance depends almost entirely on how the firm introduces it and what it asks junior lawyers to do with the output.

The traditional learning curve

In a traditional transactional training model, junior lawyers learn to draft by doing it badly, getting it corrected by a partner, doing it less badly, and repeating. The feedback loop is slow because partners are busy, the corrections happen in isolation from the original drafting decision, and the junior lawyer rarely understands clearly why the correction was made rather than what was changed.

This model produces good lawyers eventually, but it takes three to five years to produce a junior associate who can be trusted with a first draft on a standard transaction without close supervision. During that period, the associate is an expensive resource that generates limited revenue while absorbing significant partner time.

Where AI accelerates the curve

A junior lawyer who starts with a well-grounded AI draft based on the firm's precedents is immediately in review mode rather than reconstruction mode. They are asking whether the draft is right for this client and this deal, identifying departures from the instructions, spotting issues the AI has not addressed. These are judgment skills rather than formatting skills, and they develop faster because the feedback loop is tighter: the lawyer can see the firm's standard position in the draft and understand why their modification is or is not an improvement.

The firm's institutional knowledge is embedded in the draft from the outset, giving the junior lawyer access to positions and decisions that would previously have taken years of deal experience to accumulate.

Managing the risk

The risk is that junior lawyers treat AI output as authoritative rather than as a starting point that requires their judgment. A firm that allows associates to file AI drafts without substantive review is not accelerating training; it is removing it. The discipline of using AI assistance well is itself a skill that needs to be taught explicitly.
