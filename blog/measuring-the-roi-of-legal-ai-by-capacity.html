Measuring the ROI of legal AI by capacity, not cost

13 June 2026 · 3 min read

Measuring the ROI of legal AI by capacity rather than cost savings produces a more accurate and more useful picture of what an AI investment actually delivers. The cost savings framing leads firms to evaluate AI tools against a metric that is largely irrelevant to how transactional practices generate value, and it consistently underestimates the real commercial impact.

The cost savings trap

The instinct when evaluating any new technology is to look for direct cost savings: what will we spend less on as a result of this investment? In a professional services firm, this usually means looking for headcount savings, which is both the wrong frame and the wrong outcome. A transactional law firm that reduces its associate headcount because AI handles part of the drafting work has not created value; it has reduced capacity. The fee earner time that AI frees up is only valuable if it is deployed on additional revenue-generating activity.

The cost savings metric also produces numbers that look unimpressive against the investment. Saving three hours per matter across fifty matters a year is 150 hours, worth perhaps a certain multiple of an associate's hourly cost. That number is real but it is not the point.

Capacity as the right metric

The correct way to measure the ROI of legal AI in a transactional practice is to ask what additional work the same team can do in the same time. If AI-assisted drafting reduces the time a team of fifteen fee earners spends on document production by twenty percent, what happens to the remaining time? If that time is filled with additional matters, the ROI is the revenue from those matters minus the cost of the tool. If it reduces overwork without adding revenue, the ROI is reduced associate turnover and associated recruitment costs. If it is not captured at all, the ROI is low regardless of the time saving.

Making the capacity real

The question for any firm evaluating AI is whether the capacity it creates is real and whether the firm has the demand to fill it. A firm that is already turning away work because of capacity constraints, or that is growing its client base, or that is trying to handle current demand without burning out its team, is in a position to convert AI-created capacity into genuine return.
