Will AI replace junior lawyers at law firms and in-house teams?

Published 17 November 2026·5 min read
The short answer

No, AI will not replace junior lawyers, but it is already replacing a good part of what junior lawyers used to spend their days doing. Legal AI can produce a first draft, summarise a markup or pull clauses from a data room, but a lawyer still has to judge the output, and a supervising solicitor stays accountable for it. The firms and in-house teams that do well will use AI to move juniors onto judgement work sooner, not to stop hiring them.

The question comes up in almost every conversation about legal AI, from trainees on the SQE route to partners at mid-sized transactional firms and general counsel planning next year's headcount. Tools such as Harvey, Legora, Spellbook, Microsoft Copilot and Scriptum AI all automate tasks that once filled a junior's week. That changes the job. It does not remove the need for people who can do it.

Will AI replace junior lawyers?

Not in any foreseeable sense, because the parts of the job AI handles are the parts that need checking, and checking needs a lawyer. What AI removes is the blank page and the repetitive first pass.

In a transactional team, a junior's time has traditionally gone on assembling first drafts from old deals, conforming defined terms, running issues lists and comparing markups. AI drafting and review tools now do much of that first pass in minutes. The lawyer's work moves to deciding whether the draft is right for this client and this deal, which is what clients pay for.

Which junior lawyer tasks can AI already do?

AI is strongest on tasks with a clear starting document and a recognisable pattern. It is weakest where the answer depends on the client, the commercial context or a judgement call.

Who is accountable for work a junior produces with AI?

The supervising solicitor, as before. The SRA Code of Conduct for Solicitors says that where you supervise others, you remain accountable for the work carried out through them and must supervise it effectively (paragraph 3.5), and that you must make sure the people you manage are competent to do their role (paragraph 3.6).

Using AI does not change that. If anything, it raises the bar for supervision, because a confident, well-formatted draft can hide a wrong position more easily than a rough one. Our guide to the SRA professional duties and using AI covers this in more detail.

AI takes away the blank page, not the need for someone who knows what should be on it.

How will junior lawyers learn if AI does the first draft?

Only if the firm designs for it. A junior who accepts an AI draft without understanding it learns nothing; a junior who has to explain why each clause is there learns faster than one who spent the afternoon copying from an old deal.

Candidates qualifying through the SQE still need two years' full-time, or equivalent, qualifying work experience, so firms and in-house teams remain responsible for giving that experience real substance. A simple routine helps:

  1. Let the junior produce the first draft with AI, from the firm's own precedents, not generic text.
  2. Ask them to mark every clause they changed and every position they would push back on.
  3. Review the draft with them, focusing on the reasoning rather than the typing.
  4. Have them run the next markup round, with the partner setting the limits.
  5. Capture what was agreed, so the next junior starts from the firm's real positions.

We set out more on this in training junior lawyers with AI-assisted drafting.

What does this mean for in-house legal teams?

For in-house teams, AI is more likely to change what the team says yes to than how many lawyers it employs. Most in-house teams are not overstaffed; they are carrying a backlog of contracts the business wants back yesterday.

AI that drafts from the team's approved templates and handles routine markups against agreed positions lets a junior in-house lawyer clear more of that work, while senior counsel keeps the judgement calls. The risk to manage is the same as in a firm: make sure the junior understands the positions, rather than just forwarding what the tool produced.

Common questions

Will AI replace junior lawyers?

No. AI can take on much of the routine first-pass work junior lawyers have traditionally done, such as first drafts, clause extraction and markup comparisons, but a lawyer still has to judge the output and a supervising solicitor remains accountable for it.

Will law firms hire fewer trainees because of AI?

Some firms may change the shape of their intake, but the work still needs lawyers who can check, decide and advise, and the next generation of partners has to come from somewhere. In our view, the bigger change is in what juniors spend their time on rather than whether they are needed.

Is it safe for a junior lawyer to use AI to draft contracts?

It can be, if the tool drafts from the firm's own approved precedents, the junior understands every change, and a supervising lawyer reviews the work before it goes out. Under the SRA Code of Conduct, the supervisor remains accountable for work done through people they supervise.

How should firms train junior lawyers when AI writes the first draft?

Make the junior explain the draft. Have them produce the first version with AI, mark the positions they would push back on, review the reasoning with them, and let them run the next negotiation round within limits a partner sets.

Does AI change the role of junior lawyers in in-house legal teams?

Yes. AI that drafts from approved templates and handles routine markups lets junior in-house lawyers clear more of the contract backlog, while senior counsel keep the judgement calls and the relationship with the business.
