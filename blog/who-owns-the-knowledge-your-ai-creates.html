Who owns the knowledge your AI creates?

15 August 2026 · 3 min read

Who owns the knowledge your AI creates is a question that most transactional firms have not yet asked explicitly, but it is one of the most commercially significant questions in evaluating a legal AI tool. The knowledge generated through AI-assisted practice, including refined positions, improved clause language and insights about counterparty behaviour, represents a significant firm asset. Understanding who controls it matters.

The vendor dependency risk

Some AI tools are built on a model where the knowledge that accumulates through the firm's use of the product is held within the vendor's infrastructure. The firm's precedents, positions and practice patterns improve the vendor's model, which may be used across many firms. When the firm's subscription ends, the accumulated knowledge stays with the vendor. The firm has contributed to building something it does not own and cannot take with it.

This is not always explicit in the product description, but it is a logical consequence of architectures where the model improves from aggregated user data. Firms evaluating AI tools should ask directly: does the knowledge we build in this system belong to us, and can we export it if we stop using the product?

What firm-owned knowledge looks like

Firm-owned knowledge in an AI context means that the precedents, positions, clause annotations and deal records that the firm contributes to the system remain the firm's property, are held in the firm's environment or in a clearly firm-specific partition, and are not used to improve a shared model that benefits other firms. When the firm leaves the product, it takes its knowledge base with it.

This is more than a data portability question. It is a question of whether the investment the firm makes in configuring, curating and improving its AI environment builds a proprietary asset or subsidises a vendor's general product.

Why it matters for competitive advantage

The firms that invest most thoughtfully in curating their precedents and building structured deal knowledge through AI are building a compounding advantage. That advantage is only durable if the knowledge base belongs to the firm. Scriptum is built on the principle that the firm owns the knowledge it creates.
