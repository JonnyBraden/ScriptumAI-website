Ten questions to ask before you buy legal AI

3 August 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

Ten questions to ask before you buy legal AI will save your firm a difficult conversation six months into a contract that is not delivering what the sales process promised.

Where does it live in the workflow?

Does the tool integrate with Microsoft Word or does it require lawyers to use a separate application? Workflow integration is the single strongest predictor of adoption. If lawyers have to context-switch to use the tool, they will use it selectively at first and not at all within a few months. Ask the vendor for adoption data from comparable firms, not just headline customer numbers.

What is it trained on?

Internet text produces internet-quality legal drafts. Your firm's own approved precedents produce something far more useful. Establish at the outset exactly what the model uses to generate output and ask to see an example drafted from a document similar to your standard work.

Does knowledge accumulate?

Some tools produce output and forget. Others capture clause choices, negotiation positions, and deal outcomes so the base of knowledge improves over time. Understand which you are buying, and how long it takes before that accumulation becomes useful.

Who owns the knowledge it creates?

If the tool captures intelligence from your matters, that intelligence belongs to your firm. Confirm the data ownership position in writing before you sign. A vendor who hedges on this is worth treating with caution.

What does the pricing look like?

If the vendor cannot give you a price in a first conversation, that is information. Per-user transparent pricing means you can do the maths yourself without a proposal cycle. Ask whether there is a founding-firm rate and what it locks in for future users.

How long does onboarding take?

For a firm of 20 to 100 fee earners without a dedicated technology team, a six-month implementation is a material cost in time and management attention. Get a realistic timeline with specific commitments about what your firm needs to contribute and what the vendor delivers.

How does it handle confidentiality?

Is your data used to train a shared model? Where is it stored? Who can access it? These are regulatory questions as much as commercial ones, and the answers affect your professional obligations to clients.

Can you test it on real documents?

A prepared demo will look good. A test on one of your own precedents, with a real drafting task and your own lawyers doing the testing, will tell you considerably more. Any vendor who will not accommodate this is not confident in the product.

What does support look like?

For a smaller firm without an IT department, the quality of support after signing matters. What is the response time for issues? Is there a named contact? How are training and updates handled without a dedicated internal team?

What does success look like after twelve months?

Ask the vendor to define what a successful implementation looks like one year in. If the answer is vague, that is a signal. You want outcomes your team can actually track, not descriptions of how other clients have felt about the product.
