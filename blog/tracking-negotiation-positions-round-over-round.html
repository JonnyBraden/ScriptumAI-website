Keeping a record of every position your firm takes

27 July 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

Tracking negotiation positions round over round is the kind of discipline most transactional lawyers aspire to and almost none manage consistently.

Why it is hard to do manually

In theory, keeping a round-by-round record of positions is straightforward. In practice, it requires someone to log what was offered, what was rejected and what moved after each exchange, across every clause that is in play. On a complex deal with multiple workstreams and a counterparty who negotiates everything simultaneously, that is a significant administrative task on top of the substantive legal work.

What typically happens instead is that the matter partner holds the position record mentally, or it is partially reconstructed from emails when a dispute arises about what was offered in round two. Neither approach is reliable, and both create risk: the risk of conceding ground that was already given, the risk of not knowing what remains to move on, and the risk that when the matter partner leaves, the record goes with them.

What a proper position record enables

A complete round-over-round record changes the negotiation dynamic. You can see at a glance what has been offered and what has been accepted at each stage. You know what the counterparty pushed on hardest and where they backed down. You can enter each round with a clear picture of where the deal actually stands rather than where you think it stands.

Over time, a position record also contributes to counterparty intelligence. Patterns emerge across deals: what a particular counterparty always asks for, where they tend to land, what language they have accepted before. That is information with real commercial value for the next deal against the same party.

Capturing it without extra work
