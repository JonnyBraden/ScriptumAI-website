Keeping a record of every position your firm takes

31 July 2026 · 3 min read

Tracking negotiation positions round over round is one of those practices that most transactional lawyers agree matters in principle and almost none carry out systematically in practice. The tools available have not made it easy, and the deal pressure has made it easy to skip.

Why the record matters

A round-by-round record of positions is useful in at least three ways. It gives the deal team a clear picture of how the negotiation is moving during the transaction, so they can identify what is driving concessions and what is holding. It gives the partner oversight of how a deal is progressing without needing to attend every call. And after the deal closes, it feeds back into the firm's knowledge base: which of its positions held across similar transactions, which tended to give way and at what point.

Without a record, the team is navigating from memory. Memory compresses complexity, forgets the sequence of movement and does not survive a handover when a key lawyer steps away from the deal mid-stream.

What to track

A useful position record does not need to capture everything. For each significant deal point, it should note the firm's opening position, any movement in each subsequent round, the other side's corresponding positions and a brief note on what drove movement if it occurred. The record does not replace professional judgment; it supports it by making the history legible to everyone on the team.

The most valuable entries are often the ones about positions that did not move. If your firm held a particular warranty cap position across a four-round negotiation, that is meaningful data for how to approach the same issue next time.

What the record produces over time

Across dozens of transactions, a position record becomes a calibration tool. It tells you which of your standard positions are genuinely firm and which tend to soften faster than the firm intends. It shows patterns in how particular counterparties negotiate. It gives incoming lawyers on a repeat matter the full history rather than a summary that depends on who they can track down to ask.
