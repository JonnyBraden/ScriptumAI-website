Knowledge

Your firm's institutional knowledge is its real moat

22 July 2026 · 3 min read

Ask a partner what makes their firm hard to compete with and they will usually reach for the obvious answers. The client relationships. The reputation. The people. All true, and all copyable. The thing that is genuinely hard to replicate is the knowledge the firm has built up about how deals in its niche actually get done. Which clauses hold. How a given counterparty negotiates. Why the warranty schedule reads the way it does. That is a firm's real moat, and most firms are letting it leak away.

Why the knowledge never gets captured

The problem is that this expertise lives in people, not systems. It sits in a senior lawyer's memory and in tracked-changes documents scattered across inboxes. Nobody writes it down, because writing it down is a second job on top of the real one. So when a lawyer retires or moves to a competitor, the firm does not just lose a fee earner. It loses a slice of understanding it spent years paying to build.

Where generic AI tools get it wrong

This is the part the current wave of legal AI tends to miss. A general-purpose assistant will happily draft a clause for you using whatever it learned from the public internet. It gives you an answer, but it takes nothing back. None of your precedents, your positions or your outcomes stays with the firm. You end up more dependent on the tool and no richer in knowledge of your own. When the subscription lapses, you are back where you started.

The right question is not whether a tool can draft. It is whether your firm ends each matter knowing more than it did before.

Knowledge you own, captured as you work

The alternative is to treat knowledge capture as a byproduct of normal work. Every time a lawyer chooses a clause, accepts or rejects an amendment, or settles a position with the other side, that is a signal worth keeping. Recorded quietly and tied to the firm rather than the individual, those signals build into a private, searchable asset that compounds over time and outlasts the people who created it.
