Build or buy: legal AI for a small firm

27 July 2026 · 3 min read · Jonny Braden

Build or buy legal AI for a small firm is a question that sounds more complicated than it usually is. For most transactional practices with 20 to 100 fee earners, the answer is almost always buy, but the right framing matters.

What "build" actually means

Building a legal AI tool in-house means hiring engineers to fine-tune or prompt-engineer a foundation model on your firm's data, then maintaining it as the underlying models change, as your precedent bank evolves and as new practice areas emerge. It requires a technical team, a data governance process and ongoing investment that is entirely separate from the practice of law.

A handful of very large firms have done this. None of the ones doing it successfully are boutiques. The economics do not work at the scale of a 30-person practice, and the distraction from the core business is significant.

What "buy" actually means

Buying does not have to mean locking yourself into a generic point solution that forgets everything you teach it. The better question when evaluating tools is: what does the firm actually own at the end of the contract?

A tool that drafts from your own precedents and captures institutional knowledge as a byproduct of normal work is building something for you, even if the infrastructure belongs to the vendor. The firm's encoded positions, clause choices and negotiation outcomes are a proprietary asset that compounds over time and survives staff departures.

That is a different proposition from a tool that generates text from generic training data and retains nothing. Both are "buy", but only one of them builds something worth having.

The low-lift criterion

For a small firm, the implementation burden is a real constraint. A tool that requires six months of setup, a dedicated knowledge management function or a change management programme is effectively unaffordable even if the licence cost is reasonable. The right tool should fit into existing workflows, particularly Microsoft Word, with minimal disruption.

The question for a small firm is not whether to buy, but whether the tool they are buying builds institutional value or merely rents out capability. Those are not the same thing.
